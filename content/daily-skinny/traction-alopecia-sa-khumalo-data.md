Traction alopecia is not a trend and it is not rare in South Africa. Population studies led by Professor Nonhlanhla Khumalo found traction alopecia in about 17 percent of African schoolgirls and about 32 percent of African women. The cause is mechanical. Repeated pulling from tight styles, especially on chemically relaxed hair, damages follicles along the hairline until the loss becomes permanent.

## Quick answer

If the edges are thinning, the first treatment is less tension, not a new growth oil. Early traction alopecia can improve when styles loosen and, in some reported cases, when topical minoxidil is used under medical guidance. Late disease scars. CCCA is a different, scarring pattern that starts at the crown and spreads outward. Do not diagnose yourself from a reel. See a dermatologist who treats African hair if the centre of the scalp is shiny or the hairline keeps receding after you stop the tight style.

## What the South African data showed

Khumalo’s group in Cape Town measured real prevalence instead of clinic anecdotes. Hairdressing was associated with scalp disease in both schoolchildren and adults. Combining cornrows, weaves or extensions with relaxers raised the risk. Relaxers reduce tensile strength. The follicle then faces mechanical load it cannot absorb.

The fringe sign, a term Khumalo later used in public education, describes the remaining short hairs along a receding margin. It is a teaching tool so people recognise traction early, when change is still possible.

These papers are older than TikTok and still more useful than most hair-growth marketing. Prevalence in the thirties for adult women is not a niche curiosity. It is a public dermatology issue that sits next to pigment and keloids in the same communities.

## How tension actually damages a follicle

Hair that is pulled for hours, day after day, inflames the follicle. You may see bumps, broken hairs and tenderness along the braided line. Early on the follicle can recover if the force stops. Chronic injury leads to fibrosis. Once scar replaces the follicle, oils will not resurrect it.

Styles that commonly contribute include tight braids, high ponytails, heavy weaves, cornrows pulled to the scalp, and buns worn daily for work or sport. The style is not the enemy. The chronic tightness is. Protective styling can protect if it is installed without pain and given rest weeks.

Chemical straighteners, heat and traction stacked together are the worst combination in the local data. You do not have to abandon all salon services. You do have to stop treating pain during install as normal.

## CCCA is not the same disease

Central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia starts on the vertex and moves out. It is a scarring alopecia more often discussed in women of African descent. It can coexist with traction. Shiny skin, loss of follicular openings and a slowly expanding bald patch need a scalp exam, sometimes a biopsy, and a plan that may include anti-inflammatory medication.

Confusing CCCA with "my relaxer ate my hair" delays care. Confusing early traction with CCCA scares people out of useful conservative changes. A clinician who sees African scalps weekly is better than a generic trichology advert.

## What this means for your week, not your identity

Hair is culture, work presentation and time. Telling someone to "just wear it out" ignores school rules, corporate dress codes and the hours a wash-and-go can take. The practical path is lower tension versions of the styles you already use:

- Larger braids and longer install intervals
- Lightweight weaves and fewer tracks at the hairline
- Edges left out or softly curled rather than glued
- Satin scarves at night instead of elastic that cuts
- Relaxer frequency dropped, not necessarily banned overnight

Pain during braiding is a stop sign. A headache after a ponytail is a stop sign. Blood at the follicle is not a beauty tax.

## Who should pay attention

School-age girls in tight cornrows. Women who have worn weaves continuously for years. People noticing a wider forehead in photos. Anyone with a family history of crown thinning. Barbers and braiders who want to keep clients’ hairlines employed.

## Ingredients and clinic options

Topical minoxidil has limited but encouraging case reports in early traction alopecia, including work associated with Khumalo. It is not a licence to keep the tight style. Anti-inflammatory scalp treatments, antibiotics for pustular flares, and steroid lotions are clinic decisions. Biotin shampoo will not override physics.

If you use oils, choose them as comfort, not as medicine. Castor oil on a scarred margin is a ritual. It can coexist with better habits. It cannot replace them.

## South African context

Access to dermatologists is uneven. Metro clinics and private rooms in Cape Town, Durban and Johannesburg see these patterns constantly. Smaller towns rely on GPs and teledermatology. Good photos in daylight, a history of styles and chemicals, and a note of pain or itching make a remote consult more useful.

School and church dress expectations still push tight grooming. That social pressure is part of the disease ecology, the same way colourism is part of the lightening-cream ecology. A skincare desk cannot undo that in one briefing. It can say the medical bit plainly: tension plus time equals loss.

## What to do this week

1. Take a straight-on photo of your hairline in daylight.
2. Loosen or take down the tightest style you are wearing.
3. Stop adding new tracks to a thinning margin.
4. Book a dermatologist if the crown is shiny or the recession is progressing.
5. Ask your braider for less tension before you ask for a growth tonic.

## FAQ

Will my edges grow back? If follicles are still present, often partly. If the skin is smooth and shiny, often no.

Are braids always harmful? No. Chronic tight braids on weakened hair are.

Is this only a women’s issue? Men who wear tight styles and chemically treated hair can get traction too. The published school and adult figures were highest in women because of styling patterns.

Workplaces that police "neat" hair often reward the exact tension that causes traction. If you can, alternate a looser style on weekends to give follicles days off. If you cannot, reduce weight. A lighter weave for six weeks is a medical intervention disguised as a salon choice.

Children should not be a fashion experiment. The schoolgirl prevalence figure exists because styles start early. Teachers and parents can look for broken hairs along the temples after a new braid pattern. Complaints of sore scalp after sport are data.

After a relaxer, wait before installing a tight braid. The hair shaft is weaker. That is the window when traction studies found extra risk. A gentle wash, conditioner and time serve you better than an immediate sleek install for an event.

Inflammation can look like dandruff. Do not pick scabs along the hairline. Antifungal shampoos help seborrhoeic dermatitis. They do not treat traction. Using the wrong bottle for months is how people delay the only intervention that works: less pull.

If you already have a wide band of loss, camouflage options include fibres, tinted powders and well-designed wig units that do not glue on the remaining fringe. Camouflage is valid. Shame is not part of the protocol.

Research funding for African hair disorders has historically been thin relative to how common they are. That is changing slowly. Your appointment still helps a clinician document the pattern. Bring the photo from this week and one from two years ago if you have it. Progression is easier to see in pictures than in the mirror.

The bottom line matches the pigment briefing you may have read on this desk. Your hairline did not get the memo that a style was supposed to be harmless. Physics does not negotiate. Loosen first. Treat second. Admire the oil aisle last.

## Talking to a braider without a fight

You can say: please leave the front rows looser, I am keeping these in for six weeks, and I will come back if a braid hurts tonight. Pain on day one is not "it will settle." Swelling along a braid line on day two is inflammation. Take it down.

Pay for the time that careful install takes. Cheap and fast often means tighter. That economy shows up later at the hairline.

## Weaves, wig grips and gym elastics

Grips that cut the circumference of the head create a band of traction that is easy to miss because the hairline looks fine. Move the grip. Alternate with a satin-lined cap. Gym elastics on wet hair add snap. Dry, loose, low.

Glue on thinning edges trades one problem for a chemical one. If you need a unit, attach behind the fragile zone.

## Boys and low cuts

Clipper work around a receding temple can hide traction from earlier braids. Barbers can flag a widening part. A gentle conversation beats a joke about a high forehead.

## Research still needed

We need updated prevalence in 2020s styling cultures, including knotless braids marketed as safer. Safer is a claim. Tension is a measurement. Until someone measures, treat marketing as marketing.

Clinicians also need clearer pathways in the public sector so a teenager in a small town is not told to "use oil" for scarring alopecia. Teledermatology photos of the vertex and the margin, hair pulled back, help.

If you take one sentence into the salon, take this one. If it hurts, it is too tight. The study numbers from Cape Town already told us what happens when that sentence is ignored for years.

## Insurance, school photos and time

Medical aid may cover a dermatology visit more readily than a "hair clinic" package. Use that. School photos freeze a hairline in time. Compare last year's portrait if you need motivation to loosen a style.

Time off between installs is treatment. Put it on the calendar the way you put a bill there.

## What recovery can look like

Baby hairs along the margin after three months of lower tension. Less tenderness. A fringe that is not only broken shafts. Recovery is uneven. Photograph monthly. Do not weigh progress every morning in harsh bathroom light.

If recovery stalls at a smooth band, shift goals to camouflage and protection of what remains. That is still a win against further loss.

## Salon chemicals beyond relaxers

Bleach on Afro-textured hair plus traction is another stacked risk. Colour services have their place. They do not have to happen on the same day as a tight install. Protein overload from "repair" masks can make hair stiff and snap at the temple when pulled. Softness is a safety feature.

If a stylist dismisses tenderness as normal, find another stylist. The Cape Town data is on your side.

## Community myths

Olive oil does not restart a scarred follicle. Shaving the head does not automatically heal CCCA, though a short cut can make treatment and photos easier. Helmets and doeks are not the cause when the history is years of tight braids. Ask better questions of the history, not of the last accessory you wore.

A full page of myths still ends at the same instruction. Reduce tension now. See a scalp doctor if the skin is shiny.

## A practical 90-day hairline plan

Days 1 to 7: take down the tightest style. Cleanse the scalp gently. Photograph. Days 8 to 30: wear the lowest-tension option you can live with at work. No new tracks on the margin. Days 31 to 60: if baby hairs appear, protect them from glue and elastics. Days 61 to 90: review photos with a clinician if the band has not changed or the crown looks shinier.

This plan is boring on purpose. Hair cycles are slow. Anyone offering a 14-day edge miracle is selling a cream, not a follicle.

Family members will comment. You can tell them you are on a dermatology plan. You do not owe a longer explanation at Sunday lunch.
