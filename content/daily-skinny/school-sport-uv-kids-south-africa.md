The school field at 13:00 is not a gentle outdoor classroom. It is a UV workplace with kit bags, no shade and a forgotten bottle at the bottom of a backpack. South African children and the adults who stand on the sideline collect dose the same way outdoor workers do — in fragments that add up.

## Quick answer

Peak ultraviolet in this country often sits on top of first break and afternoon sport. A brim in the kit, shade for the wait, and a water-resistant SPF applied at home beat a bottle that never leaves the car. Isaacs and colleagues’ 2025 South African sun-protection review is blunt: cloth and behaviour do more than a smear you cannot reapply. Kids still need the smear. Adults on the boundary still need their own.

## Why school time is a UV shift

Ultraviolet does not wait for December. Highveld spring skies are bright, dry and high. Coastal schools get reflection off concrete and water. Winter rugby in the Western Cape still happens under a sun that burns pale necks. The clock that matters is not the term plan. It is 10:00 to 16:00, which is most of a school day.

Teachers cannot be a mobile shade structure. Grounds are designed for line of sight, not for trees. Pavilion roofs cover a sliver. The child who plays first team cricket fields for hours with a cap that is optional and a neck that is not.

Parents treat the school run as a logistics problem. It is also a photobiology problem. The same face that had a winter cream in July now sits in a queue of idling cars with a window that does not block UVA.

## What the SA literature actually says

Isaacs, Jacobs, Whitaker, Visser, Dlova, Wright, Tod and colleagues wrote sun-protection advice for the South African population that is specific to dermatoses and to this climate. Clothing first. Hats that cover. Sunglasses that are real UV filters. Shade that is planned. Sunscreen for what cloth cannot cover. Reapplication after sweat.

That stack is written for clinics and for households. It applies to a Grade 5 netball court because the physics does not change with age. Children burn at lower doses than many adults assume. Melanin-rich skin still collects UVA and visible light that drive pigment and long-term damage even when the child does not peel.

Wright’s public-health work on solar UV in South Africa has repeated the same uncomfortable fact for years: ambient UV here is high, altitude on the Highveld adds dose, and behaviour is the cheap intervention. A policy that only talks about bottles will fail the child whose bottle is at home.

## The kit bag problem

A 200 ml SPF 50 is useless in a locker. Water-resistant formulas that survive a match still need a film on skin before kickoff. Children will not reapply at half-time unless the culture is built the way water bottles were built — visible, expected, not optional.

Sticks and lotions in small tubes travel. Pump bottles stay in bathrooms. Put the product where the socks go. Teach the child to do ears, neck, parting, hands and the V of a sports shirt. Face-only is a half protocol.

Hats are kit. A peaked cap protects the nose and abandons the neck and ears. A bucket or a legionnaire flap is less fashionable and more honest. School uniform codes that ban brims on the field should be treated as a health argument, not a style argument.

## Sideline adults are not exempt

Coaches, umpires, parents on camp chairs. Two hours of Saturday sun is a shift. The person shouting from the boundary in a vest is collecting the same UVA as the child, plus years of previous Saturdays.

A team tent is PPE. So is a long-sleeve official shirt. So is a stick of SPF in the first-aid bag next to the ice pack. If the school can budget cones, it can budget shade.

## Skin of colour on the field

The myth that darker skin does not need protection still walks onto South African pitches. It is false in the Isaacs review and false in pigment clinics. Post-inflammatory marks after a burn or a scrape sit longer under this sun. Melasma in a pregnant coach does not care that the child is the one registered for the fixture.

Tinted SPF is often more acceptable on melanin-rich faces because it avoids the chalk cast and adds visible-light blocking. Children may reject tint. A hybrid: mineral or hybrid fluid that they will actually wear, plus a hat.

Keloids after a field abrasion are not a sunscreen lecture, but UV on a healing scar is how contrast deepens. Keep wounds covered. Do not put alcohol spray on raw skin and call it toughening.

## Sweat, chlorine and the second application

A morning layer applied at 06:30 is gone by second break if the child ran. Water-resistant on the label means a test in a lab, not a two-hour hockey match in Durban humidity. Reapply. If reapplication is impossible, cloth has to do more of the work.

Pool galas add chlorine. The barrier is already stripped. Rinse, bland cream, then SPF if they stay in the sun to watch the next heat. Do not stack a holiday peel on a swimmer in September.

## Eyes, lips and partings

The school-issue peak cap leaves a triangle of neck and two ears. Sunglasses that are toys are not filters. Lips burn and then peel into a cycle of flavoured balm. A stick with SPF on the mouth and the parting is the same briefing this desk already wrote for adults. Children have partings too.

## What schools can do this term

1. Make hats part of outdoor sport kit, not a civvies-day accessory.
2. Plant or hire shade on the exposed side of the field.
3. Put SPF sticks in the first-aid kit and treat them like tape.
4. Move assemblies out of full sun when the index is extreme.
5. Stop using “they’ll be fine, they’re dark” as a sentence.

None of that requires a new tender for a miracle lotion.

## Household protocol that survives a weekday

Apply at home to face, neck, ears, arms and any chest the shirt will show. Pack a stick. Pack a hat that the child will not lose on day one — label it. After sport, rinse chlorine or dust, cream, then evening whatever the dermatologist already mapped. Do not add a new acid because the face looks “dull” after a week of fields.

If the child is on a topical retinoid or oral isotretinoin, the field is a medical environment. That is a clinician conversation about timing and protection, not a Facebook group.

## Money, brands and the pharmacy aisle

The best SPF is the one that goes on. Pharmacy house brands with proper SPF and UVA labelling beat an imported boutique bottle that stays in a drawer. Check the date. Heat in a kit bag shortens a formula’s life. Replace the tube that lived in the car all summer.

Do not buy unlabelled “sunblock” from a stall. Do not use leftover lightening cream as a base under sport. This country already has a documented market in illegal hydroquinone and mercury. Children do not belong in that aisle.

## Photosensitivity and medicines

Some antibiotics, some acne medicines, some anti-inflammatories increase burn risk. The school nurse and the parent need the same list. A child who suddenly burns through a shirt needs a medical review, not a darker foundation.

Albinism and other photodermatoses need a school plan that is written down: hat, long sleeves, indoor alternatives when the index is extreme, no humiliation for the child who sits out a midday race. Isaacs’ paper explicitly frames photoprotection as disease management, not vanity.

## The spring spike

September in Gauteng is wind plus sudden bright days. Winter skin is thinner from heaters and hard water. The first hot Saturday of the term is when necks go red. Start the hat habit in the last weeks of winter, not after the first burn.

Pollen sits on this month too. Rubbing eyes with sunscreen-coated hands is how eyelids swell. Apply, wash hands, then pack the snack. Antihistamines are not a sun strategy.

## Teachers who stand outside

Duty in the quad is occupational sun. The same logic this desk used for outdoor workers applies: a serum is not PPE. A brim and a shirt are. Unions and school management can treat UV as a workplace issue without turning it into a wellness poster.

## After the match

Do not “treat” a burn with more acid. Cool water, bland cream, shade the next day. Blistering is a medical problem. Pigment that follows a burn on deeper skin will last months if you keep playing in a vest. Cover the mark. Keep the hat.

Painkillers do not reverse DNA damage. They make the child comfortable enough to go back into the same dose. Use them for comfort. Change the kit.

## FAQ

Can my child skip SPF if they wear a cap? No. Ears, neck, hands and the V of the shirt remain.

Is SPF 30 enough? Enough only if the film is present. SPF 50 water-resistant is the usual sports default here. Cloth still leads.

What about vitamin D? Casual school-day arms are not a deficiency protocol. Diet and, when a clinician tests and advises, a supplement. Do not collect a burn on purpose. That argument is a separate briefing and it still stands.

Will tinted SPF look odd on a child? Sometimes. Use an untinted fluid they accept and add the hat.

## A week that actually changes dose

Monday: apply at home, hat in bag. Tuesday: same. Wednesday: check the stick is not empty. Thursday: wash the cap. Friday: apply before the derby. Saturday: shade tent if you are the parent who always forgets. Sunday: cream on the neck that looks tight.

That is not a ten-step. It is logistics.

## Culture on the field

Mocking the child who wears a long-sleeve under the vest is how protocols die. Senior players set the picture. If the first team wears brims in warm-up, the U14s copy. Coaches who burn every weekend are teaching the wrong lesson.

Sponsors print logos on shirts. They can print them on long sleeves.

## Boarding schools and afternoon heat

A child who cannot go home at 14:00 needs product in the hostel bathroom and a rule that does not depend on one matron’s mood. Shared pumps get stolen. Individual labelled sticks survive.

Load-shedding that kills the geyser does not kill UV. Wipe-off cleanse, cream, SPF if they still have afternoon sport. Waterless days are not a reason to skip the film.

## Rural and township schools

Shade trees were cut for safety lines of sight. Corrugated roofs radiate. Budget SPF for a thousand children is a fantasy in many districts. Hats and timetable shifts are the honest tools. Department circulars that only mention sunscreen without mentioning cloth will fail the school that cannot buy a pallet of lotion.

Community clinics can still teach the stack: cloth, shade, then product when product exists.

## What improvement looks like

Fewer red necks on Monday. Fewer peeling noses in October. A child who can find the stick without a speech. A coach who sits under the tent instead of performing toughness.

You will not see a difference on a single Saturday. You will see it across a term.

## Editorial close

South African childhood is outdoor. That is a good thing. Outdoor without a plan is how a generation collects dose before they can vote. Isaacs et al. did not write a luxury pamphlet. They wrote a climate-specific protocol. Put the hat in the kit. Put the stick next to the mouthguard. Stand in the scrap of shade you actually have.

## Board meetings and sports days

The adult who forgot SPF for the child usually forgot their own. Sports day is an all-day UV event with a programme. Tents for marshals. Hats for the foundation phase even if it ruins the photograph your cousin wanted.

## Cricket whites are not SPF clothing

White cotton is cooler and is not a rated UPF shirt. It still beats a vest. Layer. A neck gaiter during long fielding innings looks serious and is serious.

## The forgotten child in aftercare

Aftercare on a hard playground at 15:00 is peak index in many towns. Staff ratios do not include sunscreen duty unless you build it. A labelled stick in the child’s bag is the only reliable object.
